There's a version of "data-driven design" that quietly turns into an excuse not to make decisions. Every choice gets deferred to "let's test it," as if testing produces answers instead of information. After enough usability sessions, you learn the difference: research tells you where you're wrong. It rarely tells you what's right.

## Testing is a filter, not a compass

A usability test can tell you, with real confidence, that users don't understand a flow, can't find a control, or misinterpret a label. Those are falsifiable claims — you watch five people fail the same task the same way, and you have a fact.

What a usability test can't tell you is what to build instead. Five different designers watching the same failed session will propose five different fixes, all consistent with the same research. The research narrowed the problem space; it didn't hand you the solution. That part is still design judgment, and pretending otherwise just launders a decision through the appearance of data.

## The cadence matters more than the method

At Salla, the bigger unlock wasn't a specific research technique — it was making usability testing a standing part of the roadmap instead of an occasional event. When testing only happens for "important" launches, it becomes a checkpoint people brace for. When it happens on a regular cadence regardless of what's shipping, it becomes ambient information that quietly improves everything, including the unglamorous features nobody would have bothered testing otherwise.

## What I actually watch for in a session

Beyond whether someone completes the task, I pay attention to:

- Where their cursor hesitates before it moves — hesitation is often a stronger signal than an outright wrong click.
- The words they use to describe what they expected. If their vocabulary doesn't match the product's, that's a content and IA problem, not just a visual one.
- Whether they narrate what they're doing unprompted. Silence during a difficult moment usually means confusion they don't have words for yet.

None of that shows up in a completion-rate chart. It's why I've never trusted a research readout I didn't watch at least part of firsthand — the summary strips out exactly the ambiguity that should inform the design decision.
